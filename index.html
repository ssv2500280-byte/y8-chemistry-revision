2.1Atoms

Atomic structure and the periodic table

ParticleRelative chargeRelative massWhere
Proton+11Nucleus
Neutron01Nucleus
Electron−1Almost 0Electron shells around the nucleus
  • Atomic number (proton number) = number of protons. It decides which element it is.
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons.
  • In a neutral atom, electrons = protons, so there is no overall charge.
  • Neutrons = mass number − atomic number. Lithium: 7 − 3 = 4 neutrons.

Electron shells

For the first 20 elements, fill the shells in the pattern 2, 8, 8, 2, innermost shell first. Write the number in each shell separated by commas.

Boron = 2,3 · Sodium = 2,8,1 · Aluminium = 2,8,3 · Calcium = 2,8,8,2

The group number tells you the number of outer-shell electrons (Group 2 → 2 outer electrons). The period (row) tells you the number of shells.

How the model of the atom changed

Dalton: solid spheres → Rutherford: tiny central nucleus → Bohr: electrons in shells. Models change when new evidence appears.

Exam answerThe atomic model may change in the future because new experimental evidence or improved technology may show something the current model cannot explain.
2.2Groups

Trends in groups

Group 1: the alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs)

  • Soft (cut with a knife), shiny when freshly cut, low density: Li, Na and K float on water.
  • All have 1 electron in the outer shell.
  • Going down the group: melting point decreases, reactivity increases.

Predicting a value: sodium sits between lithium and potassium, so predict between 181 °C and 64 °C (e.g. 100 °C). Always write the unit.

Safety precaution (give one): safety screen, eye protection, use a small piece, students stand at a safe distance.

Why reactivity increases down Group 1The atoms get bigger, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus. It is attracted less strongly and is lost more easily.

Group 7: the halogens (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂)

  • Non-metals with 7 outer electrons; they form two-atom (diatomic) molecules.
  • Going down: melting and boiling points increase, colours get darker, reactivity decreases (the opposite of Group 1).
  • Chlorine and bromine form similar molecules because they are in the same group with the same number of outer electrons.

Group 0 (or 8): the noble gases (He, Ne, Ar)

Unreactive (inert) gases with full outer shells: He 2 · Ne 2,8 · Ar 2,8,8. They don't need to lose, gain or share electrons.

2.3Bonding

Ions, ionic bonding and covalent bonding

Atoms react to get a full outer shell, like a noble gas.

Making ions

  • Metals lose outer electrons → positive ions (cations).
  • Non-metals gain electrons → negative ions (anions). Their names end in -ide: chloride, oxide.
  • The nucleus does not change. Losing negative electrons leaves more protons than electrons, so the ion is positive.
AtomChangeIonIon structure
Sodium 2,8,1Loses 1 electronNa+2,8
Magnesium 2,8,2Loses 2 electronsMg2+2,8
Oxygen 2,6Gains 2 electronsO2−2,8
Chlorine 2,8,7Gains 1 electronCl−2,8,8
Exam answerAn ionic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

Opposite charges attract; like charges repel. Two iodide ions (I−) are both negative, so they repel and cannot form an ionic bond.

Writing formulas: make the charges balance. NaCl (+1, −1) · MgO (+2, −2) · CaCl₂ (+2, 2 × −1) · Al₂O₃ (2 × +3 = +6, 3 × −2 = −6).

Covalent bonding

Exam answer (2 marks)A covalent bond is a pair of electrons [1] shared between two atoms [1].
  • Covalent bonds form between non-metal atoms.
  • A molecule is two or more atoms joined by covalent bonds. The atoms can be the same (O₂, Cl₂) or different (H₂O, CH₄).
  • In dot-and-cross diagrams, dots and crosses are outer electrons. The symbols only show which atom each electron came from. One shared pair = one single bond.

5.1Reactivity

The reactivity series and displacement

KNaCaMgZnFeCuAgAu

Most reactive (potassium) on the left → least reactive (gold) on the right. The order comes from how strongly metals react with oxygen, water and dilute acids.

RuleA more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt.

The added metal takes the place of the metal in the salt. The salt ending (sulfate, nitrate) stays the same.

zinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper
magnesium + zinc sulfate → magnesium sulfate + zinc
copper + silver nitrate → copper nitrate + silver

Iron in copper sulfate: the blue solution turns pale green and reddish-brown copper coats the iron.

"No reaction" answerSilver is less reactive than magnesium, so it cannot displace magnesium from magnesium nitrate solution. Always compare the two metals.

Displacement from oxides: carbon is more reactive than iron and zinc, so it can take oxygen from their oxides (the blast furnace: iron oxide + carbon → iron + carbon dioxide). In the thermite reaction, aluminium displaces iron from iron oxide.

A base neutralises an acid (metal oxides and hydroxides). An alkali is a base that dissolves in water. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.

Acid rain and ocean acidification damage things made of calcium carbonate, such as limestone buildings and coral.

5.4Crystals

Making copper sulfate crystals

Start with excess insoluble copper carbonate (or copper oxide) and dilute sulfuric acid.

  1. Add the solid a little at a time until some is left over. The leftover solid shows that all the acid has reacted.
  2. Filter. The excess solid stays on the filter paper (the residue). The copper sulfate solution passes through (the filtrate).
  3. Gently heat the filtrate to evaporate some water. Do not heat to dryness.
  4. Leave to cool so crystals form (crystallisation).
Exam answer (2 marks)Gently heat to evaporate some water and concentrate the solution [1]; leave it to cool so crystals form [1].

Acid + alkali (soluble reactants): add alkali until universal indicator turns green (pH 7), add charcoal to remove the indicator colour, filter, then crystallise.

Crystallisation is a physical change; it does not make a new substance.

5.5Conserve

Conservation of mass and energy

In a reaction, bonds break and atoms rearrange. Atoms are never created or destroyed.

Law of conservation of massTotal mass of reactants = total mass of products (in a closed system).
SituationMass on the balanceWhy
Reaction in a sealed flaskStays the sameNothing enters or leaves; atoms are rearranged
Carbonate + acid in an open flaskGoes downCarbon dioxide gas escapes into the air
Burning magnesium in a crucibleGoes upOxygen from the air joins the magnesium to make magnesium oxide

Worked example: an open flask weighs 103 g before and 100 g after. The 3 g is carbon dioxide that escaped. Counting the gas, the total is still 103 g.

Law of conservation of energyEnergy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred.
TypeEnergyTemperatureExample
ExothermicGives out heat to the surroundingsGoes upZinc + sulfuric acid
EndothermicTakes in heat from the surroundingsGoes downSodium carbonate + ethanoic acid

To compare energy changes, measure the temperature change: start temperature, then the highest (warming) or lowest (cooling) temperature. A rise of 8 °C is a bigger change than a fall of 5 °C. A final temperature alone, or the reaction time, is not enough.
